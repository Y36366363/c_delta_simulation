# Robust-reference profile correlation: pointwise inference and finite-sample limits

Jialiang Yao and Johan F. Hoorn

Integrated working draft for author discussion, 11 September 2026

## Abstract

Paired observations may be associated through their magnitudes of departure
from marginal references, even when signed association is not the scientific
target. We study the correlation of absolute-deviation profiles generated by
median/MAD-scaled Huber reference locations. Under fixed regular independent
and identically distributed paired laws, we derive a complete influence-function
expansion and consistent studentization, accounting jointly for reference fitting
and profile moments. A shared-sign construction has independent profiles at the
population references but can exhibit substantial fitted-profile bias and
empirical null rejection distortion. A staged comparison separates correlated
location errors that persist with population scale fixed from additional
amplification under complete median/MAD fitting. A model-specific result
identifies nonregular median estimation despite a nonzero population Huber
slope. An effective local conditioning index helps organize empirical bridge
transitions but does not provide a universal cutoff. A regular nonzero-effect
skew example also exhibits slow convergence and undercoverage. The contribution
is a pointwise inferential foundation and a worked account of finite-sample
limits, rather than a generally calibrated finite-sample procedure.

**Keywords:** generated profiles; influence function; Huber location; median
absolute deviation; weak null; finite-sample distortion.

## 1. Introduction

*Provisional: the Introduction below retains the current author-discussion
text pending Hoorn's revision. The short literature paragraph added at its
end is an integration input for author review.*

Some paired-data questions concern whether the same observational units are
unusual in two different domains. An observation far from its own group in one
variable may also be far from its group in another variable, regardless of
whether the two signed deviations have the same direction. This question
differs from ordinary signed association, equality of marginal distributions,
and general dependence.

The correlation-of-divergency construction of Hoorn (2025) approaches this
question by comparing observation-level summaries of all-to-all within-group
divergence. The present paper retains the paired-profile motivation but studies
a different construction: distance from a fitted robust marginal reference.
Replacing an all-to-all divergence profile with a reference-based profile changes
the estimand; it is not simply a new computational formula for the original
coefficient.

Our primary target is

\[
\rho_P=\operatorname{Corr}(|X-T_X|,|Y-T_Y|),
\]

where \(T_X,T_Y\) are marginal Huber location functionals whose scales are
determined by the median absolute deviation, or MAD. The references are
estimated from the same paired observations used to construct the profiles.
Consequently, the profiles are generated quantities, and their sampling
uncertainty includes the effect of reference estimation.

Robust location estimation provides the relevant methodological background
(Huber, 1964). However, a bounded location score does not make a subsequent
correlation of unbounded distances globally robust. Nor does a robust reference
ensure that its finite-sample estimate remains stable in all distributional
geometries.

The terminology must also be distinguished from earlier absolute-deviation
correlation measures. In particular, the interval-level coefficient of Stavig
(1982) is based on an absolute discrepancy between signed standardized values,
rather than the Pearson correlation of unsigned distances from two fitted
robust references. We do not claim novelty for taking absolute values, using
robust location ingredients, or applying the delta method. The contribution
concerns the complete inference and finite-sample behavior of this particular
generated-profile construction.

The paper addresses three linked questions. First, under which fixed regular
IID conditions does complete influence-function inference apply? Second, how
can linked reference errors generate misleading profile association when the
population profile correlation is zero? Third, how much of the resulting
transition can be organized by local conditioning of the nuisance equations?

These questions lead to deliberately limited conclusions. The regular theorem
is pointwise, not uniform near degeneracy. Reference fitting can cause severe
distortion, but does not always do so. The conditioning index is an explanatory
quantity, not a universal cutoff or an operational validity gate.

Related robust correlation methods also use median/MAD ingredients.
Shevlyakov and Vilchevski (2002) study robust estimation of signed bivariate
correlation; their principal-variable constructions do not define the unsigned
radius target studied here. General generated-regressor theory already
accounts for first-stage estimation in influence functions (Hahn and Ridder,
2013). Our derivation is a problem-specific application of nuisance propagation,
not a new general principle. The distinctions are therefore in the target,
the complete median/MAD/Huber and paired five-moment calculation, and the
worked finite-sample mechanism. A finite literature comparison does not
establish global priority.

## 2. Profiles, estimands, and the original construction

### 2.1 Paired observations and reference functional

Let \(Z=(X,Y)\sim P\) be one scientifically meaningful paired unit. Units
are IID when applying the regular theorem. For a continuous margin W define

\[
m_W=\operatorname{Med}(W),\quad d_W=\operatorname{Med}|W-m_W|,
\quad s_W=kd_W,
\qquad E\psi_c\{(W-T_W)/s_W\}=0,
\]

where \(\psi_c(u)=\max(-c,\min(u,c))\), \(k=1.4826\), and \(c=1.345\).
The raw MAD d and scaled MAD s are different quantities. Both sample medians
use the midpoint convention, including the median of absolute deviations.
Selected roots, their uniqueness and the numerical approximation are treated
in Appendix A and S1. The main profiles are uncapped:
\(a=|X-T_X|\) and \(b=|Y-T_Y|\).

### 2.2 Primary and secondary effects

Write \(\nu=E(ab)\), \(\mu_a=E(a)\), \(\mu_b=E(b)\),
\(q_a=E(a^2)\), \(q_b=E(b^2)\), and
\(v_a=q_a-\mu_a^2\), \(v_b=q_b-\mu_b^2\). At positive profile variances,

\[
\rho_P=\frac{\nu-\mu_a\mu_b}{\sqrt{v_av_b}},\qquad
C=\frac{\nu}{\mu_a\mu_b}=1+\rho_PCV(a)CV(b).
\]

Positive profile correlation means that units far from one reference tend
also to be far from the other; negative correlation means that large and
small reference distances tend to be paired. Zero is a weak covariance null,
not independence of the raw pair. The target is neither marginal distribution
equality, general dependence, signed association, nor complete dyadic geometry.
C is secondary because it also weights association by marginal profile CVs.
Both effects are invariant to separate positive rescaling of the profiles.
The qualification is **robust reference, not globally robust correlation**.

For \(\widehat a_i=|X_i-\widehat T_X|\) and
\(\widehat b_i=|Y_i-\widehat T_Y|\), use their ordinary sample Pearson
correlation \(\widehat\rho_P\) and
\(\widehat C=P_n(\widehat a\widehat b)/(\bar a\bar b)\).
Zero empirical profile variance is undefined correlation, not zero association.
Empirical CVs in identities below use divisor n, consistently with the moments.

### 2.3 Relation to all-to-all divergence

For this project's one-dimensional L2 implementation,

\[
D_{X,i}=\left\{\frac1{n-1}\sum_{j\ne i}(X_i-X_j)^2\right\}^{1/2},
\qquad D_{X,i}^2=\frac n{n-1}\{(X_i-\bar X)^2+s_{X,n}^2\},
\]

where \(s_{X,n}^2=n^{-1}\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2\). The identity follows by
expanding \(X_i-X_j=(X_i-\bar X)-(X_j-\bar X)\) and summing, since
\(\sum_j(X_j-\bar X)=0\). This is an exact sample identity for the stated
implementation. Its common variance floor distinguishes it from a simple
reference distance. The robust-reference C uses different profiles from the
original all-to-all coefficient. No assertion about whether a planned
normalization change has appeared in the latest public preprint is needed
for this distinction.

## 3. Complete influence-function inference

### 3.1 Reference and moment influences

Suppress the margin subscript and let \(U=(W-T)/s\),
\(A=P(|U|<c)\), \(B=E\{U1(|U|<c)\}\). At a regular law,

\[
\begin{aligned}
IF_m(w)&=\frac{1/2-1\{w\le m\}}{f(m)},\\
IF_d(w)&=\frac{1/2-1\{|w-m|\le d\}-\{f(m+d)-f(m-d)\}IF_m(w)}
{f(m+d)+f(m-d)},\\
IF_T(w)&=\frac sA\psi_c\{(w-T)/s\}-\frac BA kIF_d(w).
\end{aligned}
\]

The last term includes the median's indirect contribution through MAD.
At a symmetric population B is zero. Define
\(g_X=E\operatorname{sign}(X-T_X)\),
\(h_X=E\{\operatorname{sign}(X-T_X)b\}\), and analogously
\(g_Y=E\operatorname{sign}(Y-T_Y)\),
\(h_Y=E\{\operatorname{sign}(Y-T_Y)a\}\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
IF_\nu&=ab-\nu-h_XIF_{T_X}-h_YIF_{T_Y},\\
IF_{\mu_a}&=a-\mu_a-g_XIF_{T_X},\quad
IF_{\mu_b}=b-\mu_b-g_YIF_{T_Y},\\
IF_{q_a}&=a^2-q_a-2E(X-T_X)IF_{T_X},\\
IF_{q_b}&=b^2-q_b-2E(Y-T_Y)IF_{T_Y}.
\end{aligned}
\]

All influences are evaluated on the same pair. In moment order
\(M=(\nu,\mu_a,\mu_b,q_a,q_b)^\top\), with \(D=\sqrt{v_av_b}\),

\[
\nabla\rho_P=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/D\\-\mu_b/D+\rho_P\mu_a/v_a\\
-\mu_a/D+\rho_P\mu_b/v_b\\-\rho_P/(2v_a)\\-\rho_P/(2v_b)
\end{pmatrix},\qquad IF_\rho=\nabla\rho_P^\top IF_M.
\]

One influence value is contributed by each intact pair. Its variance retains
numerator–denominator, direct–reference and cross-margin covariances. Shared
pairwise edges cannot be treated as IID observations.

### 3.2 Pointwise theorem

**Theorem 1.** Under Conditions R1–R5 in Appendix A and the local class
verification in S1.3, at a fixed regular law,

\[
\sqrt n(\widehat\rho_P-\rho_P)
=n^{-1/2}\sum_i IF_\rho(Z_i)+o_P(1)\ \Rightarrow\ N(0,V_\rho),
\quad V_\rho=E(IF_\rho^2)>0.
\]

The sample variance of complete plug-in influences,
\(\widehat V_\rho=(n-1)^{-1}\sum_i
(\widehat{IF}_{\rho,i}-\overline{\widehat{IF}}_\rho)^2\), converges to
\(V_\rho\). Therefore
\(\sqrt n(\widehat\rho_P-\rho_P)/\sqrt{\widehat V_\rho}\Rightarrow N(0,1)\).
The Wald interval is \(\widehat\rho_P\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}
\sqrt{\widehat V_\rho/n}\).

Appendix A provides the proof route and S1 supplies its explicit Bahadur,
paired-score, entropy, density, and studentization arguments. The theorem is
**pointwise, not uniform**, and gives no minimum safe sample size. S1.7
separates its root-residual condition from the actual fixed-tolerance solver.
At global independence the reference coefficients cancel; cancellation also
occurs in some symmetric dependent profile-null laws, but not under every
weak null. For general nonzero correlation the quadratic terms require the
stronger moments in R4. The reduced C and weak-null arguments are in S1.6.

## 4. A worked finite-sample failure

### 4.1 Shared-sign construction

Let \(X=S\exp(\tau U)\), \(Y=S\exp(\tau V)\), \(\tau>0\), where
S is symmetric Rademacher and U,V are independent standard normal variables
independent of S.

**Proposition 1.** Both population medians and Huber references are zero,
the raw MAD is one, and the population profiles are independent. Thus
\(\rho_P=0,C=1\), although X,Y are dependent. The marginal density at zero
is zero. For the midpoint sample median,
\(\sqrt n|\widehat m|\to\infty\) in probability, whereas the population
Huber score slope is \(-\Phi\{\log(ck)/\tau\}/k<0\).

Appendix B proves the proposition. At \(\tau=.10\) this slope is about
\(-.67449\); the example is not explained by a nearly flat population Huber
equation. The positive median-density condition of the regular theorem fails.

### 4.2 Location error and additional fitting amplification

With population scale fixed, a Huber fit equals the sample mean whenever
all fitted residuals are in its linear region. The sample means satisfy

\[
E(\bar X\bar Y)=e^{\tau^2}/n,\quad
E(\bar X^2)=E(\bar Y^2)=e^{2\tau^2}/n,\quad
\operatorname{Corr}(\bar X,\bar Y)=e^{-\tau^2}.
\]

These follow from the intact-pair moments and independence across units.
They exhibit a direct shared-sign location-error pathway with no estimated
median or MAD. Full fitting can additionally move the median, change the MAD
about that median, and amplify the location errors. Population symmetry makes
B zero; such finite-sample scale propagation is not a nonzero first-order
MAD derivative at that law. Section 6.4 compares these paths on identical samples.

For fixed offsets and independent radii independent of S, if
\(R_X>|t_X|,R_Y>|t_Y|\) almost surely, then
\(|SR_X-t_X|=R_X-St_X\), so the induced covariance is \(t_Xt_Y\) and the
correlation is

\[
\frac{t_Xt_Y}{\sqrt{(v_X+t_X^2)(v_Y+t_Y^2)}},
\qquad v_X=\operatorname{Var}(R_X),\quad v_Y=\operatorname{Var}(R_Y).
\]

For lognormal radii this no-crossing condition does not hold globally for
nonzero offsets. With fitted sample offsets the exact no-crossing sample
identity, using divisor n, is instead

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}_n(\widehat a,\widehat b)
={}&\operatorname{Cov}_n(R_X,R_Y)
-\widehat T_X\operatorname{Cov}_n(S,R_Y)\\
&-\widehat T_Y\operatorname{Cov}_n(R_X,S)
+\widehat T_X\widehat T_Y\operatorname{Var}_n(S).
\end{aligned}
\]

The empirical cross terms cannot be discarded by inserting fitted offsets
into the population identity. Conditional on root-n reference errors and an
appropriate no-crossing approximation, radial variance of order \(\tau^2\)
gives a product contribution of correlation scale \(1/(n\tau^2)\).
Comparing that with \(n^{-1/2}\) suggests \(\tau\asymp n^{-1/4}\).
This is a **conditional rate heuristic**, not a local-to-degeneracy theorem
for the complete estimator.

## 5. Effective local conditioning

For nuisance errors in raw-MAD units and the fixed score normalization in
S1.2, the marginal derivative is

\[
J=\begin{pmatrix}
df(m)&0&0\\
d\{f(m+d)-f(m-d)\}&d\{f(m+d)+f(m-d)\}&0\\
0&-B&-A/k
\end{pmatrix},\qquad I_n=\sqrt n\,\sigma_{\min}(J).
\]

At a nonsingular fixed law the weakest standardized direction has a local
linear amplification scale \(1/I_n\). Its covariance also involves the score
covariance \(\Omega_W=E(\xi_W\xi_W^\top)\) explicitly defined in S1.2:
\(\operatorname{Var}\{(\widehat\theta_W-\theta_W)/d_W\}
\approx J^{-1}\Omega_WJ^{-\top}/n\).
The target additionally depends on its nuisance projection, profile variation
and direct–reference covariance. Matching J does not match the entire
first-order distribution. Residual differences cannot automatically be
classified as higher order.

In the unbridged model, \(J=\operatorname{diag}(0,1/(\tau\sqrt{2\pi}),-A/k)\).
Thus \(I_n=0\) for every positive \(\tau\); it cannot distinguish the severe
and diffuse controls. Separate positive-density bridge comparisons are now
provided in S2, including all 24 bridge cells and matched-index and prospective
family panels. These are empirical permutation stress results. They support
the bounded description **first-order organizer, not universal cutoff**.

## 6. Simulation evidence

### 6.1 Design, methods and Monte Carlo reporting

The roles are separated: regular-law Wald checks; nonregular-model mechanism
interventions; and supporting permutation stress comparisons in S2. All use
intact paired units. S3 specifies the exact generating distributions, seeds,
RNG consumption, retained denominators and source files. All main profiles
are uncapped. The default IF density estimates use a Gaussian kernel with
\(h=1.06\widehat\sigma n^{-1/5}\). Full fits use the public API's median-start
reweighting solver, at most 100 iterations and location-change tolerance
\(10^{-10}\max(1,\widehat s)\). S1.7 explains why this is not itself the
theorem's negligible score-residual condition.

For influence values \(\widehat\ell_i\), every main Wald standard error uses
\(\{\sum_i(\widehat\ell_i-\bar\ell)^2/[n(n-1)]\}^{1/2}\).
Intervals are ordinary untruncated two-sided 95% Wald intervals, without a
Fisher transform. Rates use valid retained replications; no failed draw is
replaced. Binomial MCSE is \(\sqrt{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)/R}\).
Wilson intervals describe Monte Carlo uncertainty. Paired contrasts use the
sample SD of within-replication differences divided by \(\sqrt R\).

## 7. Permutation scope

Re-pairing leaves both marginal samples, medians, MADs and Huber references
unchanged. It changes joint moments and the studentized statistic. The exact
fixed-orbit identity is
\(\widehat C_\pi=1+\widehat\rho_{P,\pi}CV_n(\widehat a)CV_n(\widehat b)\).
It gives matching unstudentized ordering under matching tail conventions,
not equality of arbitrary studentized p-values. Exact randomization validity
requires group invariance; the weak null alone does not provide it. Theorem 1
is not a conditional permutation CLT. S2 supplies actual identities, method
definitions, figures and supporting results under this restricted interpretation.

## 8. Discussion

The contribution is complete inference and a worked analysis of finite-sample
limits for generated robust-reference profiles. The regular theorem supplies
a pointwise foundation. The shared-sign construction shows how linked fitted
reference errors can create severe distortion; the stages distinguish a direct
location-error path from additional full-fit amplification. The regular skew
study shows a separate limit: even oracle references do not remove its
undercoverage. Numerical derivative checks and regression tests establish
neither statistical calibration nor independent acceptance of the proof.

Local conditioning explains part of a transition but must be interpreted
together with score noise, target projection, profile variation, direct–reference
covariance and nonlinear remainder. Neither a sample-size threshold nor an
index cutoff is supplied. Classical alternatives answer different scientific
questions; different null hypotheses cannot be ranked using rejection rates
alone. A bounded reference score does not make an uncapped profile correlation
globally robust.

A real application remains optional. It requires a defensible independent
unit, scientific pairing, sampling population, and documented clustering,
missingness, selection and preprocessing. No dataset has yet passed that
design review. Shared edges, repeated measures or clusters require a different
influence/resampling argument; a small number of buildings cannot be treated
as an automatic cluster-CLT justification. No application is claimed here.

S1–S3 now provide the technical argument, bounded permutation evidence and
reproducibility details used by the main text. Independent mathematical
review and clean-environment reproduction remain outstanding. Final author
decisions on placement, affiliations, journal, availability statements and
accurate AI-assistance disclosure are recorded separately from the scientific
claims. A universal cutoff, new resampling method or general degeneracy
theorem is not a prerequisite for this paper's bounded contribution.

## Appendix A. Conditions and proof of Theorem 1

**R1 (sampling).** Intact pairs are IID from a fixed law P.

**R2 (quantiles).** Each median is unique, raw MAD is positive, and the
marginal density is continuous and strictly positive in neighborhoods of
\(m,m-d,m+d\).

**R3 (nondegeneracy).** Each population Huber root is unique, its active
probability A is positive, and there is no mass at T or its clipping
boundaries. Both profile variances and \(V_\rho\) are positive.

**R4 (general-effect moments).** For some \(\eta>0\),
\(E\{a^{4+\eta}+b^{4+\eta}+(ab)^{2+\eta}\}<\infty\).
These are sufficient, not minimal, conditions.

**R5 (empirical estimation).** Selected nuisance estimates are measurable
and consistent with the stated midpoint convention. The Huber score residual
is zero or \(o_P(n^{-1/2})\). Density estimates are uniformly consistent on
fixed neighborhoods of the required boundaries. S1.5 gives the default-KDE
route and S1.7 the numerical implementation qualification.

**Local class control (the former R6).** S1.3 identifies the indicator,
Huber-score, radius, squared-radius, paired-product and weighted-sign classes;
gives polynomial covers and square-integrable envelopes under R4; and verifies
their localized IF and squared-IF GC closures, including random fitted
coefficients. This replaces a bare appeal to an unspecified R6 condition.

**Proof.** The paired Bahadur expansions and Huber Z-expansion in S1.1–S1.2
give the complete nuisance influences. S1.3 supplies random-index stochastic
equicontinuity; S1.4 verifies the population Taylor remainders and hence the
joint five-moment expansion. The ordinary correlation delta method and IID
CLT give the first conclusion. S1.5 proves plug-in population L2 consistency
and in-sample empirical variance consistency using the squared-class GC
argument. Empirical centering and Slutsky give studentization. Every covariance
is taken over intact pairs. This is a fixed-law proof, with no asserted uniform
bound on nuisance conditioning or on the remainder. The reduced C and weak-null
moment arguments are given separately in S1.6. \(\square\)

## Appendix B. Proof of Proposition 1

Let \(G_\tau\) be the CDF of \(\exp(\tau U)\). For x>0,
\(F_X(x)=1/2+G_\tau(x)/2>1/2\); for x<0,
\(F_X(x)=\{1-G_\tau(-x)\}/2<1/2\). Hence the unique median is zero and
the median of the absolute radius is one. Symmetry and the odd Huber score
give T=0. Every finite clipping interval has positive probability, so the
population score is strictly decreasing and this root is unique. Independence
of the two positive radii gives \(\rho_P=0,C=1\).

For x nonzero,
\(f_X(x)=\phi\{\log|x|/\tau\}/(2\tau|x|)\), which tends to zero at
zero; its values at the MAD boundaries are positive. On an unbalanced sign
sample, the absolute midpoint median is at least the smallest observed radius.
Thus for every fixed M>0,

\[
P\{\sqrt n|\widehat m|\le M\}
\le P(\text{exact sign balance})+nG_\tau(M/\sqrt n)\longrightarrow0.
\]

The first term is \(O(n^{-1/2})\) for even n and zero for odd n; the Gaussian
lower-tail decay in the second dominates n. Finally
\(A=\Phi\{\log(ck)/\tau\}\), giving score slope -A/k. Failure of median
regularity identifies a failed sufficient condition of Theorem 1; it does not
preclude an alternative asymptotic theory for a composite functional.
Population root uniqueness is not a guarantee of uniqueness for every finite
sample root. \(\square\)
